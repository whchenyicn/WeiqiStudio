As you improve at Weiqi, you will hear the word tenuki.

Tenuki means playing elsewhere.

Your opponent plays a move.

Instead of answering nearby, you choose a move in another part of the board.

This can be powerful.

It can also be dangerous.

The beginner challenge is learning when the local situation really needs an answer and when another area is more important.

## What Does Tenuki Mean?

Tenuki means not answering locally.

If your opponent plays in one corner and you play on the other side of the board, you have tenuki.

In simple terms:

Tenuki means choosing a different area.

It does not mean you forgot your opponent's move.

It does not mean you ignored danger.

Good tenuki means you looked at the local position and decided another move was more valuable.

## Tenuki Is Not Simply Ignoring the Opponent

Beginners sometimes think tenuki means:

My opponent played there, but I do not care.

That is not quite right.

Strong tenuki comes from judgment.

You ask:

- Is my group safe?
- Is my opponent threatening something serious?
- Is there a bigger move elsewhere?
- Can I return later?

If the local position is safe enough, playing elsewhere may be correct.

If the local position is urgent, tenuki may be a mistake.

## When Answering Locally Is Necessary

Sometimes you should answer.

Answer locally when ignoring the move would create a serious problem.

For example:

- Your group may be captured.
- Your group may be cut apart.
- Your opponent threatens atari.
- Your eye shape may disappear.
- Your territory may be badly damaged.

If your stones are weak, be careful.

Weak groups usually need attention before tenuki.

If this feels difficult, read [How to Save a Weak Group in Weiqi](/articles/how-to-save-a-weak-group-in-weiqi).

## When Playing Elsewhere May Be Better

Sometimes answering locally is too small.

Your opponent may play a move that looks annoying but does not threaten anything urgent.

If you answer every small move, your opponent may control the pace of the game.

Playing elsewhere may be better when:

- Your local group is already safe.
- The opponent's move is small.
- A larger area is open.
- You can gain more territory elsewhere.
- Your move elsewhere creates a stronger threat.

This is one reason Weiqi is not only about local fighting.

You must look at the whole board.

For opening direction, review [Where Should You Play Your First Move in Weiqi?](/articles/where-to-play-first-move-in-weiqi).

## Urgent Moves and Big Moves

Tenuki becomes easier when you understand the difference between urgent moves and big moves.

An urgent move affects safety.

A big move affects value.

For beginners, urgent moves usually come first.

If one of your groups may die, saving it is urgent.

If your opponent can cut two groups apart, defending may be urgent.

A big move might be an open corner, a large side extension, or a move that builds territory.

Big moves matter.

But if you ignore an urgent move, you may lose a group before the big move matters.

## Situation Comparison

Here is a beginner-friendly way to think about tenuki:

| Situation | Usually Answer Locally? | Consider Tenuki? |
|-----------|--------------------------|------------------|
| Your group is in atari | Yes | No |
| Your weak group has no eye shape | Usually yes | Only if another move saves more |
| Your group is already alive | Usually no | Yes |
| Opponent makes a small endgame move early | Not always | Yes |
| Large open corner remains | Depends on safety | Yes, if local danger is small |
| Opponent threatens to cut your stones | Usually yes | Be careful |
| Local move is small and gote | Not always | Often |

This table is not a rulebook.

It is a starting point.

## Why Weak Groups Usually Need Attention First

A weak group is a group that is not safe.

If you tenuki while your weak group is under attack, your opponent may make the attack stronger.

Then you may need several moves to repair the damage.

Sometimes you can still tenuki.

For example, if saving the group is too expensive, sacrificing it may be correct.

But beginners should usually check weak groups carefully before playing elsewhere.

Ask:

Can this group live, connect, or escape?

If the answer is unclear, the position may be urgent.

## Why Beginners Answer Too Many Small Moves

Many beginners answer almost everything.

This feels safe.

If the opponent plays near your stones, you reply.

If the opponent pokes at your territory, you reply.

If the opponent makes a small threat, you reply.

The problem is that not every move deserves an answer.

Some threats are small.

Some groups are already safe.

Some local positions can wait.

If you answer every small move, your opponent gets to decide where the game happens.

You lose chances to play bigger moves elsewhere.

## Why Beginners Sometimes Tenuki Too Early

The opposite mistake also happens.

Some beginners learn that playing elsewhere can be strong.

Then they tenuki before their stones are safe.

They leave weak groups behind.

They ignore cuts.

They miss atari.

They play a big move, but their earlier stones collapse.

Good tenuki requires checking safety first.

Playing elsewhere is not strong if the move you ignored was urgent.

## How Sente and Gote Relate to Tenuki

Tenuki is closely connected to sente and gote.

If your opponent plays a move that you must answer, they may have sente.

If their move does not need an answer, you may be able to tenuki.

If your own move forces a reply, you may keep the initiative and then play elsewhere next.

This is why [sente and gote](/articles/what-are-sente-and-gote-in-weiqi) are useful beginner ideas.

They help you judge whether a move really demands a response.

But remember:

Sente is not always good.

Gote is not always bad.

Tenuki is not always clever.

The question is always:

What is most important now?

## Five Questions Before You Tenuki

Before playing elsewhere, ask these five questions:

1. Is my group safe?
2. Can my opponent capture, cut, or severely damage something?
3. Is the local move urgent or just annoying?
4. Is there a clearly bigger move elsewhere?
5. If I tenuki, can I handle my opponent's next move?

You do not need perfect answers.

The habit of asking is what matters.

Over time, these questions will become natural.

## Common Tenuki Mistakes

### Tenuki while in atari

If your stones are in atari, answer unless you have a very clear reason not to.

Most beginners should simply save important stones first.

### Tenuki from a weak group

If your group has no eyes, few liberties, or no connection, playing elsewhere may be risky.

Check safety first.

### Answering every small threat

Some moves look threatening but are not urgent.

If your group is safe, look for bigger moves.

### Tenuki without looking at the whole board

Do not play elsewhere randomly.

Look for the largest and most useful area.

### Tenuki because you feel bored

Sometimes local defense is correct even if it feels quiet.

Calm moves can be strong.

## How to Practice Tenuki as a Beginner

After each game, review one moment where you answered locally.

Ask:

Did I really need to answer?

Then review one moment where you played elsewhere.

Ask:

Was my local position safe enough?

This kind of review will improve your judgment.

For a simple review process, read [How to Review Your Weiqi Games and Improve Faster](/articles/how-to-review-your-weiqi-games).

You can also compare your choices with [Common Beginner Mistakes in Weiqi](/articles/common-beginner-mistakes-in-weiqi).

## Key Takeaways

Before moving on, remember these ideas:

- Tenuki means playing elsewhere instead of answering locally.
- Tenuki is not simply ignoring your opponent.
- Answer locally when your group is weak, in danger, or likely to be cut.
- Consider tenuki when the local move is small and a larger move exists elsewhere.
- Urgent moves usually come before big moves.
- Sente and gote help you judge whether a move needs an answer.
- Before tenuki, ask whether your stones are safe.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is tenuki in Weiqi?

Tenuki means playing elsewhere instead of answering your opponent's move locally.

### What is tenuki in Go?

Tenuki in Go means the same thing as tenuki in Weiqi.

It means choosing another area of the board instead of responding nearby.

### Is tenuki bad?

No.

Tenuki can be good if the local position is safe and another move is bigger or more urgent.

### When should beginners avoid tenuki?

Beginners should avoid tenuki when an important group is weak, in atari, cut off, or unable to make eyes.

### How do I know if a move is urgent?

A move is urgent if ignoring it may cause serious damage, such as losing a group, being cut apart, or losing eye shape.
